What one build contains

  • The register. Every pair in your universe that passes the stated test, with the test statistic, the false-discovery q-value beside it, half-life, hedge ratios, the current reading, capacity, borrow breakeven and the out-of-sample checks, as one self-contained file that opens in a browser and as a table for your own systems.
  • The dossiers. The strongest candidates, several hundred per build, each open into their own page: the spread, the walk-forward hedge ratio, the setting sweep, and the caveats that apply to that pair specifically. The register lists every accepted pair; the dossiers go deep on the ones worth the depth.
  • The brief. One page on what moved since the last build: entries, exits, the pairs that lost their relationship, the ones near the boundary.
  • The method document. Every definition, threshold and control, written so that the desk is checkable from your side rather than trusted.

How it is built

Engle-Granger in both orientations against finite-sample critical values, hedge ratios by total least squares, half-lives with the small-sample correction, a held-back period for out-of-sample checks, and a false-discovery rate against a null built from real series rotated out of alignment. Capacity comes from actual dollar volume, with the leg that binds it identified per pair. Same-issuer, same-underlying and same-exposure pairs are flagged rather than hidden. The data source's survivorship gaps are measured and printed, not assumed away.

The self-test ships with it. When acceptance is recomputed inside each test year so nothing can know the year it is judged on, accepted pairs do not beat correlation-matched rejected ones, and no column ranks the year ahead. The desk is honest about that on its front page. It sells computation, freshness and correctness. And it is honest about its blind spot: a relationship that has recently died still passes the test most of the time, so the survival facts are printed per pair and nothing ranks on them.
